To understand why our planet shakes and splits, we need to travel to its heart. At the centre of the Earth, the temperature reaches about 5,400 degrees Celsius, as hot as the surface of the Sun. Like a giant engine room, this heat drives slow, massive movements of rock that crack the planet's outer shell into pieces. Earth is built in layers. At the centre sits a solid inner core of iron and nickel. Around it swirls a liquid outer core. Above that lies the mantle, a thick layer of hot rock that flows very slowly, like extremely thick syrup. And on top is the thin, rocky crust we live on. As the mantle churns, it pulls and pushes on the crust until it cracks. Those cracks divide the surface into giant, rigid slabs called tectonic plates. The North American, Eurasian, African, South American, Pacific, Australian, Indian, Arabian, Nazca and Philippine plates all drift across the mantle, a few centimetres every year. Where they meet, collide or pull apart, the Earth gets dramatic. Nowhere more so than around the Pacific Ocean. This vast horseshoe of plate boundaries is called the Ring of Fire. It holds more than 75 percent of the world's volcanoes, and it is the most violent zone of earthquakes on the planet. The Andes, Mount St. Helens and the islands of Japan were all shaped by its restless energy. Some plates are moving apart. Hidden beneath the Atlantic Ocean lies one of the longest mountain chains on Earth: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, stretching about 16,000 kilometres. Here, hot mantle rock rises, lava seeps up through the gap, and new seafloor is created. The plates are forced further apart, and the Atlantic Ocean grows a little wider every year. In one place, the ridge rises above the waves. Iceland is an island of fire and ice, largely covered by glaciers, yet built entirely by volcanoes on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Huge cracks and rifts run across the land, tracing the line where North America and Europe are slowly pulling away from each other. In 2010, one Icelandic volcano reminded the world of that power. Eyjafjallajökull blasted a plume of ash more than 11 kilometres into the sky. Winds carried the cloud across the North Atlantic toward Britain and Europe, and airports across the continent closed. For about a week, much of Europe's air travel came to a standstill.
