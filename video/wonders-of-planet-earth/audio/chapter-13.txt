Wind is nothing more than moving air. It is invisible and almost weightless. Yet no one can control it, and it is one of the most destructive forces on the planet. When winds gather into giant storms, they can grow into hurricanes more than 800 kilometres across. Air moves because the Sun heats the Earth unevenly. Warm air has more energy: it expands and rises. Cooler air sinks and rushes in to take its place. These endless flows of rising and falling air create the weather systems that sweep across our planet, and sometimes, when the conditions are right, they turn extreme. Tornadoes are born when warm, wet air collides with cool, dry air. A column of rising air begins to spin, forming a giant rotating thunderstorm called a supercell. From its base, a violently spinning funnel can reach down to the ground. As it races across the land, it sucks up dirt and debris, turning into a dark brown column of wind. Tornadoes can be short-lived, but they are terrifyingly powerful. On 3 April 1974, the United States suffered one of its worst outbreaks ever recorded. In less than a day, nearly 150 tornadoes tore across the country, killing around 300 people and injuring about 6,000 more. The greatest storms on Earth have three names. In the Atlantic they are hurricanes, in the western Pacific they are typhoons, and in the Indian Ocean they are cyclones. The strongest bring winds of more than 250 kilometres per hour, strong enough to rip the roofs off buildings, and they push walls of seawater inland in deadly storm surges. In 1900, a hurricane slammed into the city of Galveston, Texas. A storm surge more than four metres high flooded the city and flattened its buildings. As many as 10,000 people died in a single night, making it the deadliest natural disaster in United States history. More than a century later, in August 2005, Hurricane Katrina brought devastation to New Orleans and the towns around it. In the Great Plains of the United States lies Tornado Alley, and with it, a special kind of scientist: the storm chaser. Following computer weather models, storm chasers race toward developing storms to collect data. Some even drive armoured vehicles into the heart of a tornado, protected from the howling wind, torrential rain, lightning and giant hailstones.
