Rock is tough, but it is also brittle. Under the constant stirring of the Earth below, the rocky crust is pushed past its breaking point. When crunch time comes, giant blocks of rock buckle and slip, and the surface of the Earth shakes and shatters. It happens along faults: enormous cracks in the crust where two blocks of rock move in different directions. As they grind past each other, they can get stuck. Pressure builds up, year after year, until suddenly the rock gives way and jolts forward. That sudden release is an earthquake. The place deep underground where the rock first breaks is called the focus. The point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre. From the focus, seismic waves race outward in every direction, shaking the ground and causing destruction in towns and cities. Some earthquakes have changed history. In 1906, movement along the San Andreas Fault tore through San Francisco, destroying much of the city. And in 2010, an earthquake in Chile was so powerful that it shifted the entire city of Concepción about three metres to the west. In 2008, a massive earthquake struck Sichuan Province in southwest China. In Beichuan County, roads split apart, hillsides collapsed and whole towns were reduced to rubble, forcing survivors to walk out along broken roads. Earthquakes are among the deadliest natural events on Earth, and predicting exactly when one will strike is still virtually impossible. What we can do is build smarter. In Japan and California, buildings use flexible materials and shock-absorbing foundations that let them sway instead of collapse. Scientists even demolish old buildings on purpose to test steel safety cages that could protect people inside. When a fault slips beneath the sea, the whole ocean can move. A sudden jolt of the seafloor pushes up a huge volume of water and sends it racing outward as a tsunami. When the fault lies close to land, the waves can be devastating. In 2004, a mega-tsunami swept across the Indian Ocean, devastating coastlines from Indonesia to Thailand, including the Phi Phi Islands.
