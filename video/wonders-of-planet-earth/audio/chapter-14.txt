For thousands of years, strange weather was explained by myths and legends, often as signs from the gods. Today, scientists have found that most of these oddities have rare, but completely natural, causes. Take clouds. The study of clouds is called nephology, and for many people it is as much an art as a science. Clouds are made of water droplets or ice crystals, and in the right conditions they form amazing shapes: mushrooms, jellyfish, even donuts. Mammatus clouds hang like rows of pouches beneath a storm, and often warn that violent weather is on its way. For centuries, people have reported fish and frogs falling from the sky. The cause is real. Tornadoes, and their watery cousins called waterspouts, can suck animals out of lakes and rivers, carry them for kilometres, and drop them during a rainstorm. Waterspouts like this have been seen twisting above rivers such as the Mekong in Cambodia. Sometimes the Sun seems to wear a ring. Tiny ice crystals high in thin clouds bend and reflect sunlight, creating a halo around the Sun, often with bright spots on either side called sundogs. It looks like a rainbow wrapped around the Sun. Ice can also fall from the sky in dangerous sizes. During a storm in Aurora, Nebraska, in 2003, a hailstone nearly 18 centimetres across crashed to the ground. In 2010, an even bigger one, about 20 centimetres wide, fell in Vivian, South Dakota. Around the world, lightning strikes about 100 times every second. Lightning can even flash inside the ash cloud of an erupting volcano. But one form remains a mystery: ball lightning. These glowing balls, sometimes the size of a peach, have been reported floating through windows and walls, hissing, and even exploding. High above thunderstorms, other strange lights flicker: blue jets and red sprites, flashes that are probably caused by electrical activity in the upper atmosphere. And sometimes the Moon itself looks blue. When forest fires or volcanoes fill the sky with tiny particles, they scatter moonlight and give the Moon an eerie blue tint. Floods are among the most common natural disasters on Earth, but flash floods strike with almost no warning. After unusually heavy rain or sudden melting of ice, water rises in minutes and swamps dry land. In 2010 and 2011, floods in Queensland, Australia, covered an area larger than France and Germany combined.
