Rivers can grind walls of rock into dust. They carry millions of tonnes of soil and sand, and hold enough energy to power entire cities. There are long rivers, wide rivers and deep rivers. But by almost any measure, one river stands above them all: the Amazon. Its story begins high in the Andes Mountains, where water from glaciers and snowfields trickles down the slopes. Mountain streams such as the Urubamba River join together, forming the headwaters that will become the greatest river on Earth. As it crosses South America, the Amazon is fed by more than 1,100 tributaries, more than any other river in the world. Some arrive as thundering waterfalls, like those that carry water down from the Guiana Highlands. The Nile is usually considered slightly longer. But in volume, the Amazon leaves it far behind. Every second, the Nile pours about 5,100 cubic metres of water into the sea. The Amazon pours about 220,000, around 43 times as much. In a single day, that is enough fresh water to supply a city for ten years. The next biggest rivers, the Congo, the Yangtze, the Yenisei and the Paraná, are not even close. In the wet season, the Amazon's floodwaters spread across an area about the size of Germany. Near its mouth, the river can be up to 40 kilometres wide. Its waters feed the largest rainforest on Earth, covering around five million square kilometres. Altogether, the Amazon holds about 20 percent of all the river water flowing into the world's oceans. Every year, seasonal rains flood the lowlands around the river, creating vast floodplains of forest standing in water. In these slow, muddy channels lives one of the Amazon's most mysterious animals: the river dolphin, which hunts fish and crabs in water so murky that scientists still know little about its life. The Amazon also carries a huge load: around three million cubic metres of sediment into the ocean every day. And where the river meets the sea, the incoming tide pushes back. Long waves called tidal bores, or pororocas, roll upstream, and surfers can ride a single wave for up to ten kilometres.
