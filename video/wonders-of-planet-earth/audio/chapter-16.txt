At the top of the world lies the Arctic: a frozen ocean surrounded by continents. In the height of summer, the Sun never sets, and daylight lasts for 24 hours. In winter, there is at least one day when the Sun never rises. Is there land beneath the North Pole? In 1958, a submarine sailed all the way under the frozen Arctic Ocean and proved there is not. The polar ice cap is floating on water. Life here is tough, but some animals thrive. Polar bears hunt seals on the sea ice, and Arctic foxes often follow them, feeding on the scraps the bears leave behind. Beneath the ice, divers have discovered a surprising world of creatures living at the very edge of what life can bear. The cold can be brutal. In northern Greenland, temperatures can drop to about minus 70 degrees Celsius. At the other end of the Earth lies Antarctica. Unlike the Arctic, it is a continent, buried under the largest ice sheet on Earth. Its ice covers about 14 million square kilometres, one and a half times the size of the United States, and holds about 70 percent of all the fresh water on the planet. Antarctica may look empty, but its seas are full of life: penguins, seals, whales and albatrosses. All of them depend on krill, tiny shrimp-like animals that live in the Southern Ocean in such huge numbers that they are among the most abundant animals on Earth. In winter, the ice sheet spreads into the ocean as floating ice shelves. Giant blocks break away to become massive icebergs. In parts of Antarctica, the Sun stays below the horizon for 105 days in winter, leaving the land in near-total darkness. And it was here, at Vostok Station, that the coldest temperature ever recorded was measured: minus 89.2 degrees Celsius. Scientists still explore its ice caves to uncover the secrets of the continent's past.
