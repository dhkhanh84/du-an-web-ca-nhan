When India crashed into Asia, the collision pushed up the greatest mountain range on land: the Himalayas. In geological terms they are young, just 50 million years old, and still rising. At their crown stands the highest mountain on Earth. In Tibet it is called Chomolungma. In Nepal, Sagarmatha. The rest of the world knows it as Mount Everest. Its modern story began with surveyors. In 1841, the British survey of India recorded the position of a remote summit known simply by a letter. In 1852, calculations showed it was taller than any mountain ever measured. In 1865, it was renamed after Sir George Everest, the former Surveyor General of India. Measuring the exact height of Everest has caused years of debate. Snow, ice and different survey methods all change the answer. In 2020, China and Nepal announced a joint figure: 8,848.86 metres above sea level. For decades, the summit seemed impossible. In 1924, a British expedition climbed to about 8,570 metres before turning back. Then, on 29 May 1953, Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay of Nepal became the first people to stand on top of the world. Since then, thousands have followed, including a retired Japanese teacher who reached the summit in 2007 at the age of 71. The most popular route starts in Nepal and climbs the Southeast Ridge. At Base Camp, on the Khumbu Glacier at 5,400 metres, climbers spend weeks letting their bodies adjust to the thin air. Just above lies one of the deadliest sections of the climb: the Khumbu Icefall, a maze of shifting ice towers and deep crevasses, crossed with ropes and aluminium ladders. Above the icefall, climbers move up through a chain of camps: Camp 1 at about 6,000 metres, Camp 2 at 6,500, Camp 3 at 7,500, and Camp 4 at 8,000 metres. They scramble with ropes over a band of black rock called the Geneva Spur, surrounded by giant neighbours: Lhotse and Nuptse. Above 8,000 metres lies the Death Zone. Here, each breath holds only a third of the oxygen found at sea level, and the cold can freeze skin in minutes. The human body cannot survive here for long. Climbers use ice axes and crampons, metal spikes on their boots, to cling to walls of ice on the final push to the summit. Sadly, Everest now holds another title: the world's highest garbage dump. Climbers and tourists have left behind plastic bottles, food packaging and empty oxygen tanks. And Everest is not alone up there. The five tallest peaks on Earth are Everest, K2 at 8,611 metres, Kangchenjunga at 8,586, Lhotse at 8,516 and Makalu at 8,485.
