Deep beneath our feet lies a hidden world. Dark caverns and winding tunnels stretch far into the Earth, their walls decorated with strange stone shapes. For the brave explorers who venture down, the rewards are some of the most awesome sights on the planet. Most caves are carved from limestone, and limestone has an extraordinary origin. Its main ingredient, calcium carbonate, comes from the shells and skeletons of ancient sea creatures. Over millions of years, they piled up on the seafloor and were pressed, layer by layer, into solid rock. When rainwater soaks through the ground, it picks up carbon dioxide and becomes slightly acidic. This weak acid slowly dissolves limestone, hollowing out tunnels and chambers. Inside the cave, dripping water leaves its minerals behind. From the ceiling grow icicle-shaped stalactites. From the floor, stalagmites rise to meet them, often just a fraction of a millimetre a year. Sometimes, the ground above a hollow simply gives way. When weakened rock collapses into the cavern below, a sinkhole opens, often without warning. In Guatemala, during a tropical storm, a sinkhole about 30 storeys deep suddenly swallowed a three-storey building. Caves are among the least explored places on Earth, which is exactly why cave explorers, called spelunkers or potholers, find them so thrilling. But every expedition carries real risks: collapsing rock, freezing water, falls, sudden floods, and getting lost in the dark. In Gunung Mulu National Park on the island of Borneo, cave experts have mapped more than 320 kilometres of passages. Here lies the Sarawak Chamber, the largest known cave chamber by area: about 700 metres long and 100 metres high. Nearby, Deer Cave was discovered only in 1976. And in Vietnam, Hang Sơn Đoòng is so vast it has its own jungle and clouds. It is the largest cave passage ever found. The longest cave system on Earth is Mammoth Cave, in Kentucky. Explorers have now mapped more than 686 kilometres of its passages, and they are still finding more.
