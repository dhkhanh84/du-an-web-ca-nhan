Soft, fluffy snow can turn a landscape into a stunning white wilderness. But in its most extreme forms, snow becomes a killer. From raging blizzards to avalanches that strike with the force of solid rock, the power of snow should never be underestimated. Snow forms in cold air, mostly near the poles or high on mountains. Water droplets freeze into tiny ice crystals, and the crystals stick together to form snowflakes. The simplest snowflake is a six-sided prism, but it can grow branches into beautiful, complex shapes. Its final form depends on the temperature and the amount of water in the air. Is every snowflake unique? Probably, although no one could ever check them all. With trillions upon trillions of crystals falling every year, the chance of two identical ones forming in the lifetime of the Universe is virtually zero. Some places get more than their share. In the winter of 1998 to 1999, Mount Baker in Washington State, in the United States, recorded a total snowfall of 29 metres, about as tall as a ten-storey building. When strong winds drive heavy snow, a snowstorm becomes a blizzard: winds of at least 48 kilometres per hour and visibility of less than 200 metres. In a severe blizzard, called a whiteout, you cannot see your own hand. In 1949, a season of blizzards struck the western United States and lasted seven weeks. More than 100 people and around a million cattle died. Avalanches are even more sudden. In 1999, after heavy snowfall near Chamonix in France, about 300,000 cubic metres of snow broke loose from a mountainside. The avalanche raced down at almost 100 kilometres per hour and slammed into a small village, burying homes under 100,000 tonnes of snow. Twelve people died. Mountain rescuers have simple advice. Before you go, check the avalanche forecast and carry a rescue beacon. You cannot outrun an avalanche, but you may be able to move to its side. If you are knocked down, grab a tree or a rock, and "swim" to stay near the surface, because the snow sets like concrete. When you stop, clear a space in front of your face so you can breathe. And if you see someone swept away, mark the spot where you last saw them.
