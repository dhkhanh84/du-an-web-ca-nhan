From far away, it looks calm. A blue and white marble, turning quietly in the dark. But up close, our planet never sits still. Mud bubbles. Winds twist the air into spinning spirals. The ground cracks open. Earth was born 4.6 billion years ago, and it has been boiling, freezing and reshaping itself ever since. Welcome to the wonders of planet Earth. And Earth is not alone. Every day, small lumps of rock and metal from space, called meteoroids, slam into our atmosphere. Friction heats them until they glow. The bright streaks they leave behind are what we call meteors, shooting stars, or, when they are big and bright enough, fireballs. In November 1833, the skies over the United States put on the most brilliant meteor shower ever recorded. Thousands of glittering lights rained down through the night. People were woken from their sleep, and many ran outside convinced that the world was coming to an end. Sometimes, the visitor is far bigger. About 66 million years ago, a giant space rock struck near Chicxulub, in what is now Mexico. The impact hurled so much dust and rock into the sky that sunlight was blocked and the climate was thrown off balance for thousands of years. Scientists believe most animal species died out, including the dinosaurs. What if it happened again? A giant meteorite would heat the air beneath it to around 60,000 degrees Celsius, ten times hotter than the surface of the Sun. It could blast a thousand cubic kilometres of rock and gas into the air, and send shock waves through the ground strong enough to trigger earthquakes. The scars of such impacts still mark our planet. The largest known crater, at Vredefort in South Africa, is about 300 kilometres across. Not every visitor from space is destructive. Some are breathtaking. Near the North and South Poles, the night sky sometimes fills with ghostly curtains of green and violet light: the aurorae. They appear when streams of particles, blown from the Sun on the solar wind, collide with Earth's magnetic field and light up the upper atmosphere. Astronauts on the International Space Station can watch them ripple below. Then there are comets: chunks of ice, rock and frozen gas that blaze with light as they approach the Sun. In 1996, Comet Hyakutake passed just 15 million kilometres from Earth and became one of the brightest comets seen in two hundred years. It is now heading back to the edge of the Solar System, and no one alive today will see it again. It will not return for tens of thousands of years.
