On our restless planet, nothing stays the same for long. Landscapes are always being carved, worn down and reshaped. A human life is far too short to watch it happen. But the results are all around us. In dry places, the wind becomes a sculptor. It lifts grains of sand and hurls them against rock like a sandblaster. In Arches National Park in Utah, this slow attack has carved natural stone arches, balancing rocks and flat-topped mesas that stand like monuments in the desert. Sometimes the wind leaves strange shapes behind. Hoodoos are tall spires of rock with a cap of harder stone on top, protecting the softer rock beneath. In Australia, the Devils Marbles are giant round boulders, sandblasted over time until they seem to balance on top of one another. Water can be an even stronger carver. When fast-moving water rushes through a crack in soft rock, it can cut a deep, narrow channel with smooth, curving walls: a slot canyon, glowing with light that filters down from far above. The greatest rock carver of all is the Colorado River. Over millions of years, it has cut the Grand Canyon: 446 kilometres long, up to 29 kilometres wide, and in places more than 1.6 kilometres deep, exposing rocks close to two billion years old. Its power comes from its steep slope, the huge amount of mud, sand and gravel it carries, and the relatively soft rock it flows through. Along the coast, the ocean never rests. Waves crash against the rocks, armed with sand and grit that grind the cliffs away. Over time, the base of a cliff is undercut until the rock above collapses into the sea. In Australia, the Painted Cliffs were carved this way, their layers stained orange and red by minerals.
