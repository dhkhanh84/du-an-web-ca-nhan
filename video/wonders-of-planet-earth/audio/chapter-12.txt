The deep ocean is the least explored place on our planet. It is pitch-dark, freezing cold, and the pressure could crush a submarine. Yet even here, life finds a way, from colossal squid with eyes bigger than a human head, to fish that make their own light. As we dive, the ocean changes. In the sunlit zone near the surface, there is enough light for plants to grow. Below about 200 metres begins the twilight zone, where only a faint glow remains. Past 1,000 metres is the dark zone. Below 4,000 metres lies the abyssal zone. And deeper than 6,000 metres, in the ocean trenches, is the hadal zone, one of the most extreme and least known places on Earth. In the darkness, it snows. Tiny bits of dead plants and animals, called marine snow, drift down from the waters above. Over thousands of years, they settle on the seabed and build up layers of fine mud and ooze, some of them up to 450 metres thick. The creatures of the deep look like they come from another world. The Dumbo octopus flaps ear-like fins as it hovers above the seafloor, thousands of metres down. Giant ostracods, relatives of tiny shrimp, glide through the dark water like floating lanterns. On the ocean floor, cracks in the crust release volcanic heat. These hydrothermal vents, also called black smokers, gush mineral-rich water as hot as 400 degrees Celsius. Instead of sunlight, life here runs on chemicals. Blind shrimp, starfish, tube worms and fish gather around the vents like an oasis in a desert. The deepest point of any ocean is the Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench in the Pacific. In 1960, two explorers, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh, climbed into a submersible called the Trieste and sank nearly 11 kilometres to the bottom. It was one of the most dangerous journeys ever attempted. For more than fifty years, they were almost alone. Filmmaker James Cameron made a solo dive in 2012, and since 2019 a new submersible has carried more explorers down. By 2022, 27 people had reached the Challenger Deep. That is still only a little more than the 12 people who have walked on the Moon, nearly 400,000 kilometres away. Because the deep sea is so dangerous for humans, scientists now explore it with robots. Remotely operated vehicles are steered from ships on the surface, while autonomous vehicles navigate the seabed on their own. With sonar, they map underwater mountains and valleys we have never seen with our own eyes.
