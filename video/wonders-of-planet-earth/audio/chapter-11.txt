Earth's crust is a treasure chest. Many things we use every day, from soft talcum powder to the graphite "lead" in a pencil, were formed deep inside it. Getting them out takes enormous effort, human courage and clever technology. Our buildings are made of it. Marble is strong and easy to carve, and has been used for some of the most famous buildings in history. Iron, mixed with other elements, becomes steel. Aluminium is light and strong. Gypsum, when heated, becomes plaster for walls and chalk for blackboards. Our machines run on it. Copper bends easily and carries electricity better than almost any other metal. Nickel goes into coins and batteries. Mercury, the only metal that is liquid at room temperature, was used for centuries in thermometers. And the phone in your pocket depends on it too. A mineral called coltan is used to make mobile phones. But much of it comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where miners often dig by hand in dangerous ground, risking mudslides, toxic fumes and collapsing pits, all in conditions of extreme poverty. Some minerals are simply beautiful. Fluorite glows with intense colour under ultraviolet light. Hematite, ground into powder, became the red paint used in ancient rock art. And gold can be hammered thinner than any other metal: a single gram can be beaten into a sheet of gold leaf a square metre wide. The most precious of all is diamond. Amazingly, it is made of pure carbon, the same element as the soft graphite in your pencil. Arranged differently, carbon becomes the hardest natural mineral on Earth. The Imperial Crown of India holds more than 6,000 diamonds. Other elements are far less stable. Potassium is so reactive that it is never found pure in nature, and it bursts into flame on contact with water. Reaching Earth's buried treasure can be extremely dangerous. On 20 April 2010, the Deepwater Horizon oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico exploded. Eleven workers were killed. The rig had been drilling beneath about 1,500 metres of water, a task some engineers compared to exploring the Moon. Around five million barrels of oil poured into the sea, causing the largest accidental oil spill in history.
