In the coldest places on Earth, snow falls but rarely melts. Layer upon layer piles up, and the weight squeezes the fluffy snow into dense, solid ice. Slowly, under the pull of gravity, this ice begins to move downhill. These giant rivers of ice are glaciers, and as they creep, they scour a path through the land. At the front of a glacier, the ice can break away with a thunderous crack. In Argentina, the Perito Moreno Glacier pushes into Lago Argentino, the country's largest lake. Huge towers of ice crash from its wall into the water, sending waves across the lake. A glacier is always melting and freezing, changing with the time of day, the season and the climate. When more ice forms than melts, the glacier grows and advances. When more melts, it shrinks and retreats. In Alaska, the enormous Hubbard Glacier has been advancing for more than a hundred years. Cruise ships that sail up to its face look like toys. Most glaciers move only a few centimetres or metres a day. But some are surprisingly fast. The Jakobshavn Glacier in Greenland is one of the fastest on Earth, flowing at up to 20 metres a day as it pours ice into the sea. When glaciers and ice sheets reach the ocean, great chunks break off and float away as icebergs. Because ice is less dense than water, about 90 percent of an iceberg hides below the surface. That hidden danger has always threatened ships. In 1912, an iceberg sank the Titanic, and more than 1,500 people lost their lives. For some animals, though, icebergs are useful. In the Southern Ocean, colonies of penguins hitch rides on floating ice, resting and travelling until hunger sends them diving back into the sea to catch fish. Earth's climate has always changed, and ice ages are a normal part of its history. During the last ice age, so much water was locked up in ice that sea levels fell by around 120 metres. Woolly mammoths grazed on cold grasslands, hunted by sabre-toothed cats. Today, the opposite is happening in many places. The ice cap on Mount Kilimanjaro in Africa is melting so fast that scientists warn it could disappear within decades.
