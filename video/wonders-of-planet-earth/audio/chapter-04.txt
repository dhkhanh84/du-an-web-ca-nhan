The ground splits open. Molten rock, ash and toxic gas burst out with unimaginable force. A volcanic eruption is a window into the heat and pressure hidden far beneath our feet. Scientists rate eruptions on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, a scale that runs from "gentle" all the way up to "mega-colossal". So what builds the ultimate volcano? Deep underground, molten rock called magma gathers in a deep chamber fed by the mantle. It rises into a shallower chamber just beneath the surface. From there, heat and pressure force it up a main pipe, the conduit, toward the summit vent and crater. Sometimes it breaks out sideways through cracks in the flank, called side vents. When the pressure finally becomes too much for the crust to hold, the energy is released all at once. In 1980, Mount St. Helens in the United States exploded so violently that its whole north side collapsed, and the mountain lost about 400 metres of its height in a single day. Rock that has melted into a glowing, semi-liquid state is called lava. How fast it flows depends on its temperature, its minerals and the slope of the ground. The fastest lava ever measured poured from Nyiragongo, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, at more than 60 kilometres per hour. When lava reaches the ocean, it boils the seawater into clouds of steam. Some eruptions are almost beyond imagination. Nearly two thousand years ago, a volcano in New Zealand blasted out about 100 cubic kilometres of rock in one of the most violent eruptions ever recorded. The giant crater it left behind is now filled with water. Today, it is Lake Taupo. Ash can be just as deadly as lava. In AD 79, Mount Vesuvius in Italy shot a column of ash and pumice 33 kilometres into the sky, at a rate of about 1.5 million tonnes every second. When the column collapsed, it buried the nearby Roman cities, including Pompeii, under a suffocating blanket of ash. Every eruption leaves its mark. The crater is the opening of the vent. Layer after layer of lava and ash builds up around it, creating the classic cone shape. If the walls collapse, the crater grows wider. On the islands of Hawaii, some of the most active volcanoes on Earth breathe steam and gas from their craters almost every day, slowly building new land.
