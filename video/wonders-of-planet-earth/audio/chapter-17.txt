Deserts are the driest places on Earth. Years can pass without a single drop of rain. Many swing between scorching days and freezing nights. Surprisingly, even icy Antarctica counts as a desert. Without water, very few living things can survive. The giant of the deserts is the Sahara. It covers an area about the size of the United States, and it is still growing. Its sand dunes can rise as high as 300 metres, and its winds whip up sandstorms and spinning dust devils. Dust is lighter than sand, and it can travel thousands of kilometres. Sometimes, the desert comes to town. In Sudan, a fast-moving wall of sand and dust called a haboob can roll across the land and swallow a whole market in minutes, turning day into an orange darkness. Which is the hottest place on Earth? For 90 years, the record belonged to El Azizia in Libya. But in 2012, the World Meteorological Organization investigated and threw that record out. Today, the official title belongs to Death Valley, in California, where the temperature reached 56.7 degrees Celsius on 10 July 1913. Places such as Ghadames in Libya and Kebili in Tunisia have also baked at around 55 degrees. And the driest? In the Atacama Desert of Chile, the town of Arica once received less than one millimetre of rain over 59 years. In Egypt, the towns of Asyut and the Dakhla Oasis may see only one shower every few years. Yet people have built a glittering city in the middle of a desert. Las Vegas sits in the Mojave Desert and is home to millions of people, with tens of millions of visitors every year. Almost all of its water comes from the Colorado River through Lake Mead. Scientists warn that the city is growing faster than its water supply, and that one day the taps could run dry. In dry lands, fire is never far away. Wildfires are often started by lightning or by people, and after a long drought, they spread at terrifying speed. Australia sees tens of thousands of bushfires every year, and some can race through 20 kilometres of bush in a single hour. In 1997, after a severe drought, fires in Indonesia burned across 7,500 square kilometres. From fireballs falling out of space to the frozen darkness of the poles, from the roaring heart of a volcano to the silent floor of the deepest ocean, our planet is anything but still. It is boiling, shaking, freezing and changing every single day. And the more we explore it, the more we realise how much of it we are still only beginning to understand. Thank you for watching.
